Ashoka YSP-TDCS'25

Why the best education starts with better questions.

VAVivaan Agarwal 26 June 2025 4 min read Education

When I was selected for Ashoka University's Young Scholars Program, I expected to learn new things. What I did not expect was to rethink how learning itself works. Like most students, I had spent years believing that education was primarily about finding the right answers. School rewards accuracy, preparation, and certainty. The better you are at solving problems, the more successful you tend to be. The Young Scholars Program challenged that idea. For the first time, I found myself in an environment where the most valuable people were not necessarily the ones with the best answers. They were the ones asking the most interesting questions.

Before attending the program, I viewed learning as something fairly linear. You studied a subject, understood the concepts, practiced enough questions, and gradually improved. That approach worked well in school. But the people I met at Ashoka seemed to approach knowledge differently. They were constantly connecting ideas across disciplines. A conversation about technology would turn into a discussion about ethics. A session on data science would lead to questions about economics or public policy. Instead of treating subjects as separate boxes, they treated them as parts of a larger system. That was a completely different way of thinking from anything I had experienced before.

The program itself reflected that philosophy. We explored topics ranging from computer science and data science to cryptography and computational thinking. On paper, these seemed like technical subjects. What surprised me was that the discussions were often less about the technology itself and more about the ideas behind it. Learning about encryption, for example, was not just about understanding how information is protected. It raised questions about privacy, trust, security, and the role technology plays in society. The most memorable moments were rarely when I learned a new concept. They were when a concept forced me to look at a familiar problem differently.

That experience exposed a contradiction in the way many students approach education. We spend years optimizing for answers, but the real world rewards questions. Exams are designed around problems that already have solutions. Outside the classroom, the most important challenges are often the ones where no clear solution exists. Innovation does not come from applying existing knowledge perfectly. It comes from exploring problems that have not been fully understood yet. Looking back, I think one of the biggest mistakes ambitious students make is assuming that intelligence is measured by how much you know. Increasingly, I think it is measured by the quality of the questions you ask.

Of course, knowledge still matters. Curiosity without discipline does not lead very far. The students who impressed me most at Ashoka were not simply curious people. They were people who combined curiosity with rigorous thinking. They asked difficult questions and then spent time searching for meaningful answers. That balance is important. It is easy to romanticize curiosity, but curiosity only becomes valuable when it is paired with effort. What the program showed me was not that knowledge is unimportant. It showed me that knowledge is often the starting point rather than the destination.

The most lasting impact of the program was the way it changed how I think about learning itself. Since then, I have become much more interested in exploring connections between subjects. My growing interest in economics, entrepreneurship, finance, and technology all stem from the same realization: the most interesting problems rarely belong to a single discipline. Economic systems are influenced by psychology. Businesses depend on technology. Public policy shapes markets. The boundaries we create between subjects are often far less important than the relationships between them. Once I began seeing those connections, learning became far more interesting.

When people ask what I learned at Ashoka, it would be easy to list the topics we studied or the skills I developed. But that misses the bigger lesson. The most valuable thing I took away from the program was a new perspective on knowledge itself. I arrived expecting to collect answers. I left realizing that progress often begins with better questions. Since then, I have become less interested in memorizing information and more interested in understanding systems, ideas, and relationships. Looking back, the Young Scholars Program did not just teach me new concepts. It changed the way I think about learning, and that lesson has stayed with me long after the program ended.
