India Pays China $99 Billion to Compete with China

India funds the rival it is trying to compete with. An analysis of India's trade deficit with China, industrial policy and domestic manufacturing dependence.

VAVivaan Agarwal 15 June 2026 5 min read Economics

Every year, India sends nearly $100 billion across the border to China, for the parts and materials that go into products it then labels "Made in India." In FY 2024-25, India's trade deficit with China hit $99.2 billion [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India], the highest ever recorded. To put that in perspective, $99.2 billion is roughly the size of Kenya's entire economy. India is sending nearly a hundred billion dollars a year to the exact country it has declared it wants to stop depending on.

This blog argues that the Ministry of Commerce and Industry's industrial policy is generating investment but not reducing import dependence, because it is building the last step of the supply chain while China dominates many of the upstream stages before it. A supply chain is the sequence of steps that goes into making a product: mining the materials, producing the components, assembling the finished good. India has gotten reasonably good at the last step. China controls a large share of everything before it. For ordinary Indians, this matters because a country that only does final assembly captures a smaller share of the value created by the product, which means fewer stable jobs, less technical expertise built over time, and a real vulnerability every time China decides to cut off supply.

China accounts for roughly 30% of India's total import bill [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India], making it the single largest source of goods Indian factories depend on. Nearly one in every three dollars India spends on imports goes to China. What India is importing is not consumer luxuries. It is the raw material of industry itself: electronics components, active pharmaceutical ingredients that go into medicines, solar equipment, industrial machinery. Every iPhone assembled in India adds to export figures, but when most of the chips, displays, and batteries inside it are imported first, a large share of the value created still leaves the country. The $99.2 billion [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India] is not just a trade statistic. It is a measure of exactly how deep that dependency runs.

The government's answer to this problem is the Production Linked Incentive scheme, or PLI, a programme that pays companies financial rewards based on how much they produce in India. It has delivered real results. PLI has generated Rs. 16.5 lakh crore in total sales [DPIIT], created 12 lakh direct jobs [DPIIT], and helped electronics exports grow 32.46% in FY2024-25 [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India]. These are not small numbers. But PLI has done far less to support component manufacturing than it has for final assembly. India still imports over 85% of the components that go into its electronics [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India]. The chips, the screens, the batteries still come from abroad. PLI has made India better at putting things together. It has barely touched the harder question of making the things that go inside.

Solar panels show exactly what that costs. China did not just decide to sell solar panels to the world. Over 20 to 30 years, with direct state support, it built the entire chain: processing raw silicon, producing wafers, manufacturing cells, and finally assembling panels. Today it dominates every single stage. India, meanwhile, has ramped up panel assembly under PLI but still imports the cells and wafers that make those panels work, mostly from China. India puts the final product together. China built everything that made the final product possible. That gap, between assembling and actually making, is the entire problem in one example.

The government would reasonably push back. Apple manufacturing in India is real. A 32.46% rise in electronics exports is real [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India]. FDI inflows are at record levels. Building component industries takes time and supply chains cannot be remade overnight. That is a legitimate defence. But the trade deficit with China is not holding steady while India takes its time. It is growing. $99.2 billion [Ministry of Commerce, Government of India] is the highest number ever recorded. If the whole point was to reduce dependence on Chinese imports, the data says it has not worked yet.

If the problem is that India assembles products but imports their components, then industrial policy needs to move upstream, meaning closer to where the raw materials and components are actually produced. The first step is extending PLI incentives specifically to component manufacturing, chips, batteries, and display panels, with a higher incentive rate than what is currently offered for final assembly. Right now the policy financially rewards the easier part more than the harder part. That needs to flip. The second step is a time-bound import substitution roadmap for the fifteen Chinese product categories that make up the bulk of the deficit, with annual targets that are public and independently tracked.

India is not incapable of making things. The problem is that we have spent years perfecting the final step while the harder, more valuable steps stay abroad. We keep winning the last lap of a race China has already finished. Until industrial policy addresses the full supply chain and not just the final assembly line, the contradiction at the heart of India's manufacturing strategy will remain: every year, India will spend billions trying to compete with China while sending billions more to China to make that competition possible.
