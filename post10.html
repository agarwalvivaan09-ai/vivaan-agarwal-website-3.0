The Week Kaushik Basu Changed How I Think About Economics

A week that changed how I approach economic problems.

VAVivaan Agarwal 15 January 2026 4 min read Economics

Before attending the Kaushik Basu Scholars Programme, I thought economics was primarily about finding answers. Why do countries grow? Why do markets fail? Why does inflation rise? The subject seemed like a collection of theories designed to explain the world. The more I learned, the more answers I accumulated. But during that week, something unexpected happened. I left with more questions than answers. And strangely enough, I think that is what made the experience so valuable. For the first time, I began to see economics not as a body of knowledge, but as a way of thinking.

What struck me immediately was how different the discussions felt from a typical classroom environment. In school, there is usually an expectation that every question has a correct answer waiting to be discovered. Economics at the programme felt very different. Intelligent people could look at the same problem and arrive at entirely different conclusions. One person would argue for greater government intervention. Another would argue for stronger market mechanisms. Both would have evidence. Both would have logic. And neither would necessarily be completely right or completely wrong. That realization challenged something I had unconsciously assumed for years: that complexity eventually disappears if you study a problem deeply enough. In reality, some problems remain complex no matter how much you learn about them.

One discussion in particular stayed with me. We explored the relationship between economic growth and development. Before that week, I often treated the two concepts as interchangeable. If an economy was growing, development would naturally follow. The data, however, painted a more complicated picture. Some countries achieved impressive growth without solving deeper issues related to education, healthcare, or inequality. Others improved living standards despite relatively modest growth rates. The distinction seems obvious in hindsight, but it fundamentally changed how I think about progress. Growth matters, but growth alone does not tell the full story. Economic success is not just about producing more. It is about improving lives.

The programme also exposed me to a style of thinking that I found incredibly compelling. Instead of immediately asking whether a policy was good or bad, discussions often began with a different question: what are the incentives? That simple shift changed everything. Policies do not operate in isolation. People respond to them. Businesses respond to them. Governments respond to them. The outcome of a policy is often shaped as much by human behavior as by the policy itself. Once I started looking at problems through the lens of incentives, many economic issues became easier to understand. More importantly, they became harder to oversimplify.

Another lesson came from interacting with students from different backgrounds and perspectives. Everyone arrived with different interests, experiences, and assumptions. Yet we were often discussing the same questions. Why are some regions more prosperous than others? How should governments balance efficiency and equity? What makes institutions effective? Listening to different viewpoints forced me to recognize how much my own thinking was shaped by the information and experiences available to me. Economics became less about defending a position and more about understanding competing perspectives. That mindset has stayed with me ever since.

Of course, the programme did not suddenly turn me into an economist. If anything, it made me more aware of how much I still had to learn. But that was precisely the point. The most valuable educational experiences are not the ones that make you feel knowledgeable. They are the ones that make you curious. By the end of the week, I found myself reading more articles, exploring more research, and paying closer attention to economic debates than ever before. Not because I had to, but because I genuinely wanted to understand the questions being discussed.

Looking back, the Kaushik Basu Scholars Programme marked an important turning point in my intellectual journey. It moved economics from being an academic interest to something much deeper. It taught me that the most interesting questions rarely have simple answers, that incentives often matter more than intentions, and that understanding a problem is usually harder than solving it. Most importantly, it showed me that economics is not really about money, markets, or statistics. It is about understanding how people, institutions, and incentives interact to shape the world around us. That insight has influenced almost everything I have studied since.
