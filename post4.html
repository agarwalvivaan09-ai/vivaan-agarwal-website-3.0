Why I started taking economics seriously.

When economics became more than a school subject.

VAVivaan Agarwal 4 July 2025 4 min read Economics

For most of school, economics was just another subject. I enjoyed it, I scored well in it, and I found some of the concepts interesting. But I never thought about it beyond the classroom. Economics existed inside textbooks, exam papers, and neatly labelled chapters. It felt structured and predictable. Then, somewhere around the middle of 2025, that changed. I stopped seeing economics as a school subject and started seeing it as a way of understanding the world. Looking back, I think that was the moment I began taking economics seriously. The shift was not caused by a single lecture or a particular book. It happened because I gradually realized that many of the questions I was already interested in were, at their core, economic questions.

At the time, I was involved in a variety of activities. Through ElevateX, I was working with teams, organizing events, and thinking about how organizations grow. I was becoming increasingly interested in entrepreneurship and how businesses create value. At the same time, I was paying more attention to the news, trying to understand why governments made certain decisions and why countries developed at different speeds. What surprised me was that economics seemed to sit underneath all of these topics. It connected individual choices to larger outcomes. It explained why incentives matter, why markets behave the way they do, and why policies often produce results that nobody originally intended. The more I explored, the more I realized that economics was not one subject among many. It was a framework for understanding systems.

The first idea that genuinely fascinated me was that people respond to incentives. On the surface, it sounds obvious. Of course people respond to rewards and consequences. But once I started applying that idea to real-world situations, I saw it everywhere. Businesses respond to profits. Consumers respond to prices. Politicians respond to voters. Investors respond to expectations. Even organizations and nonprofits respond to incentives, whether they realize it or not. Many problems that seemed complicated suddenly became easier to understand when viewed through that lens. Instead of asking why something happened, I began asking what incentives encouraged it to happen. That shift completely changed how I approached problems.

Another reason I became interested in economics was because it challenged simple explanations. Before studying it seriously, I often viewed issues in terms of good decisions and bad decisions. Economics taught me that reality is usually more complicated. Policies that sound beneficial can produce unintended consequences. Markets that appear irrational often have deeper explanations. Well-intentioned actions can sometimes create worse outcomes than doing nothing at all. The world became less straightforward but more interesting. Rather than providing easy answers, economics forced me to ask better questions. Why do housing shortages persist? Why do financial bubbles keep appearing? Why do some countries grow rapidly while others remain stagnant? Those questions felt far more engaging than memorizing definitions for an exam.

Of course, some people see economics as a narrow discipline focused on money, markets, and statistics. I used to think that too. But the deeper I went, the more I realized that economics is ultimately about human behavior. Markets are made up of people. Governments are made up of people. Businesses are made up of people. Every model, graph, and theory is really an attempt to understand how people make decisions under constraints. That is what makes economics both powerful and imperfect. It provides useful frameworks, but it can never fully predict human behavior. The challenge lies in balancing theory with reality. Understanding that tension made the subject far more compelling than I had expected.

What accelerated this interest was exposure to people who thought deeply about these ideas. Through programs, discussions, articles, and books, I encountered perspectives that went far beyond the school curriculum. I realized that economics was not just about understanding existing systems. It was also about questioning them. Some economists argued for greater government intervention. Others believed markets should be left largely alone. Some focused on growth. Others focused on inequality. There were rarely simple answers. What mattered was the quality of the reasoning and the evidence supporting it. That intellectual debate was something I found incredibly appealing. For the first time, I was studying a subject not because I had to, but because I genuinely wanted to understand it.

Looking back, I think the biggest reason I started taking economics seriously is that it gave me a way to make sense of the world around me. It helped explain the businesses I was interested in, the organizations I was helping build, and the policies I was reading about. More importantly, it taught me to think in terms of systems rather than isolated events. A fundraiser, a startup, a government policy, or a financial crisis might appear completely unrelated on the surface. Economics revealed the connections between them. At some point, I stopped studying economics because it was part of my syllabus. I started studying it because it helped me understand how the world actually works. That was the moment it became more than a subject.
