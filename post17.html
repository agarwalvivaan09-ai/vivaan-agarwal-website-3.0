Why Economic Crises Repeat

Patterns in financial systems and human behaviour.

VAVivaan Agarwal 13 April 2026 4 min read Economics

Every economic crisis feels unique when it happens. The headlines are different. The countries are different. The industries are different. Yet when you look closely, the underlying pattern is often remarkably familiar. The Great Depression, the 2008 financial crisis, the cryptocurrency crash, the dot-com bubble, and countless other economic collapses seem unrelated on the surface. But they all followed a similar path. Opportunity attracted capital. Capital encouraged risk-taking. Risk-taking created excess. Excess eventually became unsustainable. The details changed, but the structure remained the same. This raises an important question: if we have experienced so many crises before, why do they keep happening?

The common explanation is that people forget history. Each generation, according to this argument, repeats the mistakes of the one before it. There is some truth to that idea, but I do not think it fully explains the problem. Information about past crises is widely available. Economists study them. Investors discuss them. Governments analyze them. We know what happened during previous collapses. Yet new crises continue emerging. The problem is not a lack of knowledge. The problem is that the incentives which create crises often remain unchanged. People respond to opportunities in predictable ways, and those responses can collectively create instability even when individuals are acting rationally.

The cryptocurrency boom of 2020–2022 is a useful example. The original idea behind cryptocurrencies had genuine value. Decentralized financial systems, digital assets, and new approaches to transactions represented meaningful innovations. But as money poured into the sector, the conversation began shifting. Investors became less interested in utility and more interested in price appreciation. Projects with limited practical applications reached extraordinary valuations. People entered the market because they believed prices would continue rising. For a while, that belief became self-fulfilling. More buyers pushed prices higher, which attracted even more buyers. The cycle continued until confidence weakened. Once confidence disappeared, the system unraveled surprisingly quickly.

A similar dynamic appeared in the startup ecosystem during the era of extremely low interest rates. Capital was abundant and relatively cheap. Investors were willing to fund ambitious growth strategies because money was easy to access. Startups prioritized expansion, market share, and user acquisition. Profitability often became a secondary concern. As long as funding remained available, the model appeared sustainable. When interest rates rose and investment slowed, many businesses discovered that growth alone was not enough. The environment changed, and the assumptions supporting the system changed with it. What once looked like strength suddenly appeared fragile.

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of economic crises is that they often develop during periods of optimism. People associate crises with panic, but panic usually arrives at the end of the cycle, not the beginning. The early stages are often characterized by confidence. Asset prices rise. Investments perform well. Economic indicators appear positive. Success encourages more risk-taking because recent experience suggests that risks are being rewarded. Over time, individuals begin assuming that current conditions will continue indefinitely. That assumption is what creates vulnerability. Stability becomes dependent on circumstances remaining exactly the same. When conditions inevitably change, the adjustment can be severe.

Of course, not every economic downturn becomes a crisis. Economies are constantly adjusting to new information, changing consumer preferences, technological developments, and external shocks. Most of these adjustments are manageable. Crises occur when systems become excessively dependent on assumptions that prove incorrect. Excessive borrowing, unrealistic expectations, speculative behavior, and poor risk management all contribute to this process. None of these factors are new. They appear repeatedly because they are rooted in human behavior rather than specific industries or technologies. Markets change. Human psychology changes much more slowly.

Looking back, I think economic crises repeat because the forces that create them are deeply embedded in the way people make decisions. When opportunities appear attractive, capital flows toward them. When success becomes visible, confidence grows. When confidence grows, risk-taking increases. These responses are natural, which is precisely why they are difficult to eliminate. The goal is not to create a world without crises. That is probably impossible. The goal is to understand the patterns that make crises more likely and build systems resilient enough to withstand them. Economic history is not a sequence of unrelated disasters. It is a recurring lesson in how incentives, expectations, and human behavior interact. The names change. The pattern rarely does.
