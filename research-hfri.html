Household Financial Resilience Index

How big a financial shock can a household absorb? HFRI turns savings, debt, emergency cash and spending stability into a single, transparent score from 0 to 100.

01Question

The research question

Income alone does not show how well a household can cope with a bad month. Two families on the same salary can be in very different positions: one holds several months of cash and little debt, the other has almost no buffer and heavy repayments. HFRI treats resilience as the capacity to absorb a financial shock while still meeting essential obligations.

Can a transparent mathematical index combining savings, debt, emergency liquidity and expenditure stability provide a meaningful measure of household financial resilience?

02At a glance

At a glance

The research had two phases. Phase I built the scoring functions, checked how the index behaves internally and tested how sensitive rankings are to the weights. Phase II compared HFRI with an ONS-style liquidity benchmark and put the simulated households through an income shock and a spending shock.

03Model

How the score is built

Five indicators, each converted to a 0–100 score by a smooth function fitted to calibration anchors drawn from published landmarks. Higher is always more resilient.

HFRI = 0.25·S + 0.25·D + 0.25·E + 0.25·X

D = 0.40·DDTI + 0.60·DDSTI

S savings strength, D debt sustainability, E emergency coverage, X expense stability. Equal weights are a transparent baseline, not a claim that every dimension matters equally in every household.

S

Savings Strength

SR = (Y − C) / Y S = 100·(1 − e−5.89681·(SR + 0.10)), SR > −0.10; else 0

Rewards a surplus with diminishing returns. Y is annual disposable income, C is annual spending.

D

Debt Sustainability

DDTI = 100·e−0.0573534·DTI2.13509 DDSTI = −521.45598·DSTI² + 9.08245·DSTI + 100.00523

Debt hurts twice: through the size of the stock (DTI) and the repayment burden today (DSTI). Repayments carry the larger weight because they hit monthly cash flow directly.

E

Emergency Coverage

EC = Liquid savings / Monthly essential spending E = 100·(1 − e−0.37028·EC0.901391)

Counts only money reachable quickly. The first months of cover add the most protection. Three months is used as a landmark, not a pass mark.

X

Expense Stability

CV = σ(monthly spending) / mean(monthly spending) X = 100·e−3.13957·CV1.30837

Spending that swings widely is harder to plan around. This is the most exploratory dimension, because lumpy spending is not always a sign of weakness.

How well the curves fit

The fitted curves reproduce the anchor points closely (R² between 0.9876 and 0.9997). That only describes how well each curve matches points chosen by the researcher. It does not show that a score corresponds to a real probability of financial distress. The 9 and 12 month emergency-coverage anchors are researcher-defined extensions, not literature thresholds.

04Testing

What the research tested

The simulation includes positive and negative monthly cash flows, borrowing, debt servicing, savings accumulation and borrowing constraints. It uses synthetic data, not real household records.

PHASE I

Build and stress the index

Developed the scoring functions, examined internal behaviour and tested how much household rankings depend on the weighting scheme. Rankings stayed highly correlated with the baseline.

PHASE II

Benchmark and shock-test

Compared HFRI with an ONS-style liquidity benchmark, then applied income and expenditure shocks. In the simulation, shock-absorption capacity shifts broadly around an HFRI of 65–70. These are model-based readings, not universal thresholds.

06Limits

What HFRI is not

The paper’s own conclusion is that HFRI is coherent and transparent, not yet validated. Being clear about that is part of the research.

  • The households are simulated. They are not a representative sample of Indian families, so the score distribution describes the model, not a population.
  • Anchors and weights are researcher-defined. They are informed by central bank and survey literature but not estimated from observed financial outcomes. Equal weighting is a modelling choice, not an established optimum.
  • The shock test is partly circular. Liquid savings feed both the Emergency Coverage score and the shock-survival check, so some of the link between HFRI and survival is built in.
  • Expense Stability is exploratory. A volatile budget is not always a weak one.
  • The calculator goes beyond the paper. Applying the formulas to a real household’s numbers, and the cash-flow stress test in the calculator, are extensions that have not been tested against real outcomes.
  • It is not financial advice. A score is a way to compare financial positions, not a verdict on whether a household is safe.

Next step: test HFRI against real household data and see whether low scores go with missed payments, borrowing and forced asset sales.
