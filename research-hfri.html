Household Financial Resilience Index

How big a financial shock can a household absorb? HFRI turns savings, debt, emergency cash and spending stability into a single, transparent score from 0 to 100, now tested on more than 12,000 real families.

01Question

The research question

Income alone does not show how well a household can cope with a bad month. Two families on the same salary can be in very different positions: one holds several months of cash and little debt, the other has almost no buffer and heavy repayments. HFRI treats resilience as the capacity to absorb a financial shock while still meeting essential obligations.

The first stage of the research built the index and tested it on 1,000 computer-generated households. The second stage asked the harder question: does it work on real families?

Does HFRI correctly identify real families who are in financial trouble today, and real families who will get into trouble in the future? And if its original equal weights are not the best ones, what should they be?

02At a glance

At a glance

The revised study tested HFRI on more than 12,000 real U.S. households from three Federal Reserve surveys. The surveys record each family's finances and, separately, whether it was in trouble: behind on loan payments, using payday loans, going bankrupt or refused credit. One survey interviewed the same families in 2007 and again in 2009, so the index could also be tested on trouble that came after the 2008 crisis.

03Model

How the score is built

Five indicators, each converted to a 0–100 score by a smooth function fitted to calibration anchors drawn from published landmarks. Higher is always more resilient.

HFRI = 0.05·S + 0.30·D + 0.55·E + 0.10·X

D = 0.40·DDTI + 0.60·DDSTI

S savings strength, D debt sustainability, E emergency coverage, X expense stability. The first version used equal weights of 0.25. The revised weights come from testing every combination in 5% steps against real financial distress.

S

Savings Strength · 5%

SR = (Y − C) / Y S = 100·(1 − e−5.89681·(SR + 0.10)), SR > −0.10; else 0

Rewards a surplus with diminishing returns. Y is annual disposable income, C is annual spending.

D

Debt Sustainability · 30%

DDTI = 100·e−0.0573534·DTI2.13509 DDSTI = −521.45598·DSTI² + 9.08245·DSTI + 100.00523

Debt hurts twice: through the size of the stock (DTI) and the repayment burden today (DSTI). Repayments carry the larger weight because they hit monthly cash flow directly.

E

Emergency Coverage · 55%

EC = Liquid savings / Monthly essential spending E = 100·(1 − e−0.37028·EC0.901391)

Counts only money reachable quickly. The first months of cover add the most protection. Three months is used as a landmark, not a pass mark.

X

Expense Stability · 10%

CV = σ(monthly spending) / mean(monthly spending) X = 100·e−3.13957·CV1.30837

Spending that swings widely is harder to plan around. This is the most exploratory dimension, because lumpy spending is not always a sign of weakness.

How well the curves fit

The fitted curves reproduce the anchor points closely (R² between 0.9876 and 0.9997). That only describes how well each curve matches points chosen by the researcher. The link between scores and real financial distress comes from the survey tests below. The 9 and 12 month emergency-coverage anchors are researcher-defined extensions, not literature thresholds.

04Testing

What the research tested

Each version of the index was judged on two jobs: recognising families in trouble now, and predicting which families would get into trouble later. The two jobs pulled the weights in opposite directions.

STAGE 1

Build and simulate

Built the five scoring curves and tested the index on 1,000 simulated households observed for 12 months. HFRI correlated 0.873 with an ONS-style liquidity benchmark, and rankings barely changed under moderate changes to the weights.

STAGE 2

Trouble now

On SCF 2025, the original index scored an AUC of 0.736 for being badly behind on payments, and still cut the odds of trouble by about 30% per 10 points after accounting for income, age and family size. Emergency savings mattered most.

STAGE 3

Trouble later

On the 2007–09 panel the result flipped. Debt was the strongest warning sign of trouble after the crisis, and the full index beat emergency savings alone. Savings explain trouble now; debt explains trouble later.

STAGE 4

Reweight and check

All 1,770 weight combinations were tested and the choice was checked on held-out families. 5/30/55/10 is practically tied with the best and almost exactly the average of the weights chosen in ten fair tests.

05Score bands

What a score means

The bands are set where the chance of being two months or more behind on a loan falls to twice the U.S. average (8.2%), to the average, and to half the average. They were then confirmed on families followed through the 2008 crisis.

0 – 35

High risk

13.4% of families. 20.8% badly behind in 2025; 15.1% newly behind by 2009.

35 – 55

Elevated risk

30.0% of families. 12.6% badly behind in 2025; 8.4% newly behind by 2009.

55 – 70

Moderate risk

15.8% of families. 7.2% badly behind in 2025; 4.9% newly behind by 2009.

70 – 100

Low risk

40.8% of families. 1.3% badly behind in 2025; 1.0% newly behind by 2009.

07Limits

What HFRI is not

Testing on real families answered the biggest open question from the first version, but the research is still clear about what a score can and cannot tell you.

  • The evidence is American. The weights and bands come from U.S. families. HFRI uses ratios, so it can be applied anywhere, but it has not yet been tested on Indian household data.
  • Some survey measures are stand-ins. Spending stability and the 2007 saving habit come from simple survey questions rather than month-by-month records.
  • One crisis. The “trouble later” test comes from 2007–09. A different kind of shock, such as fast-rising prices, could shift the balance between savings and debt.
  • A small trade-off. On unseen families, the revised weights are slightly worse than equal weights at predicting future trouble (about 0.01 AUC), in exchange for a much larger gain in recognising trouble now.
  • Prediction, not cause. A low score predicts trouble. It does not prove that raising the score would prevent it.
  • It is not financial advice. A score is a way to compare financial positions, not a verdict on whether a household is safe.

Next step: test the revised HFRI on Indian household data.
