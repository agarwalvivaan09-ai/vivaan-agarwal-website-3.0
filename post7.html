My First Crisis as CEO

Most people imagine leadership is tested during big moments. A major financial problem. A public controversy. A crisis that threatens the future of the organization. I used to think the same thing. Then my first real leadership crisis came from something much smaller: a disagreement. Looking back, what surprised me most was not the disagreement itself. It was how quickly a minor issue became a major problem because of the way I handled it. That experience taught me an uncomfortable lesson. Leadership is not tested by the challenges you face. It is tested by how you respond to them.

At the time, ElevateX was growing quickly. More people were joining, more initiatives were being planned, and responsibilities were becoming increasingly distributed. As organizations grow, communication becomes more important because decisions are no longer being made by one person. During this period, there was a misunderstanding between me and my CFO regarding the handling of certain financial decisions. On its own, the issue was not particularly serious. It was the kind of disagreement that could have been resolved through a conversation. Unfortunately, I was focused on being right rather than resolving the problem. Instead of addressing the issue privately, I reacted publicly.

That decision changed everything. What should have remained a discussion between two people suddenly became visible to the rest of the organization. Team members became aware of the tension. People started forming opinions. A simple misunderstanding evolved into a leadership issue. The irony was that neither of us was trying to create conflict. We were both trying to protect what we believed was best for the organization. Yet because the disagreement was handled poorly, the consequences extended far beyond the original issue. Eventually, the conflict led to him stepping away from his role. In that moment, I thought I had won an argument. In reality, the organization had lost something much more valuable.

The experience revealed a contradiction that many young leaders misunderstand. We often assume leadership means confronting problems directly and decisively. Sometimes it does. But leadership is not about winning every disagreement. It is about protecting the long-term health of the team. Looking back, I realized I had approached the situation as an individual defending my position rather than a leader protecting a relationship. The distinction sounds small, but it changes everything. Organizations are built on trust, and trust is far more difficult to rebuild than it is to damage. A decision that feels justified in the moment can create consequences that last far longer than the original problem.

Fortunately, the story did not end there. After things settled, I reached out to him privately. We spoke honestly about what had happened, clarified the misunderstanding, and addressed the frustrations on both sides. Most importantly, I apologized. That conversation taught me something that the conflict itself never could. People are often far more willing to resolve issues than we assume. What prevents resolution is usually not disagreement. It is ego. The desire to be right often becomes stronger than the desire to solve the problem. Once that happens, communication breaks down and relationships suffer. Leadership requires putting the solution ahead of your pride.

The situation also taught me the importance of unity within leadership teams. Every organization has internal disagreements. Different perspectives are healthy and often necessary for good decision-making. But there is a difference between disagreeing internally and appearing divided publicly. The moment leadership looks fragmented, uncertainty spreads throughout the organization. People begin choosing sides. Trust weakens. Stability disappears. Strong organizations are not the ones without disagreements. They are the ones that know how to handle disagreements without allowing them to damage the broader team.

Looking back, this remains one of the most valuable leadership lessons I have ever learned. Not because the crisis was large, but because it exposed weaknesses in my approach that I needed to address. It taught me to separate problems from people, to prioritize relationships over ego, and to remember that being a leader means thinking beyond the immediate moment. Today, when I hear the word "crisis," I think differently. A crisis is rarely defined by the problem itself. It is defined by how leaders respond when things go wrong. My first crisis as CEO did not teach me how to avoid mistakes. It taught me how to grow from them. That lesson has stayed with me far longer than the disagreement that first revealed it.
