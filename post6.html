What Managing People Taught Me

Leadership lessons from building a team.

VAVivaan Agarwal 15 September 2025 4 min read Leadership

Before I started leading teams, I thought leadership was mostly about making decisions. The image I had in my head was simple: a leader sets the direction, people follow, and results happen. It sounded straightforward. Then I found myself managing dozens of people through ElevateX, events, projects, and different initiatives. Very quickly, I realized that leadership had far less to do with making decisions and far more to do with managing people. And people are complicated. Everyone has different motivations, expectations, strengths, and ways of working. What looked simple from the outside turned out to be one of the most challenging skills I have ever had to learn.

The first lesson was that leadership is not really about control. It is about responsibility. When something goes wrong in an organization, people naturally look toward whoever is leading it. They do not care whether the problem was caused by a volunteer, a department head, or circumstances outside anyone's control. They want stability. Early on, I sometimes saw leadership as having authority over others. Over time, I realized it was the opposite. Leadership meant becoming responsible for problems I did not personally create. It meant stepping forward when things went wrong rather than looking for someone to blame.

One of the biggest challenges was handling conflict. Whenever groups of people work together, disagreements are inevitable. Different personalities bring different opinions, and different opinions eventually create friction. My instinct in the beginning was to solve every issue immediately. If there was tension, I wanted it resolved. If there was a disagreement, I wanted a decision. What I learned was that not every problem requires an immediate reaction. Sometimes the best response is patience. People need time to cool down, reflect, and communicate properly. A rushed solution often creates more problems than it solves. Leadership is not just about knowing when to act. It is about knowing when not to act.

Another lesson came from trust. The moment people believe you are treating them unfairly, leadership becomes much harder. In any organization, there will be people you naturally get along with better than others. There will be friends, familiar faces, and people whose opinions you value more. But leadership demands a level of consistency that personal relationships cannot influence. The moment team members feel that decisions are based on favoritism rather than fairness, trust begins to disappear. Once trust is lost, it is extremely difficult to rebuild. I learned that people do not expect every decision to make them happy. They simply expect decisions to be made fairly.

What surprised me most was how important listening became. Before leading teams, I assumed leadership involved speaking, directing, and motivating. Those things matter, but listening matters more. Some of the most valuable feedback I have ever received came from people who disagreed with me. Not all criticism is pleasant, and not all feedback is delivered politely. But if you ignore criticism because it feels uncomfortable, you lose access to information that could help you improve. Over time, I learned to separate the delivery of feedback from the value of the feedback itself. The people who challenged my decisions often taught me more than the people who agreed with them.

Of course, managing people is not only about solving problems. It is also about understanding motivation. One mistake many leaders make is assuming everyone is driven by the same things. Some people care about recognition. Others care about impact. Some want responsibility. Others want opportunities to learn. A system that motivates one person may completely fail with another. The most effective leaders are not those who force everyone into the same mold. They are the ones who understand what different people value and create environments where they can contribute effectively. Leadership becomes easier when you stop trying to manage everyone the same way.

Looking back, managing people taught me far more about myself than it did about others. It forced me to become more patient, more self-aware, and more thoughtful about my decisions. It exposed weaknesses I did not know I had and challenged assumptions I had never questioned. Most importantly, it changed my understanding of leadership itself. I no longer see leadership as the ability to direct people. I see it as the ability to create an environment where people can do their best work, even during difficult moments. Titles, authority, and positions matter far less than I once thought. In the end, leadership is not about being in charge. It is about being responsible for something larger than yourself.
