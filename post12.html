Becoming Editor-in-Chief

For years, I thought leadership was something you earned. Work hard, stay consistent, contribute meaningfully, and eventually people recognize your efforts. Becoming Editor-in-Chief of Fiscal Focus seemed like the natural outcome of that process. After all, I had spent years writing, editing, and helping the magazine grow. When I was finally appointed Editor-in-Chief, I expected to feel a sense of achievement. And I did. But what surprised me was how quickly that feeling disappeared. Within a few days, the title stopped feeling like a reward and started feeling like a responsibility. That was the moment I realized that leadership positions are often misunderstood. They are less about recognition for what you have done and more about accountability for what comes next.

Fiscal Focus played an important role in shaping my interest in economics. Through writing articles, researching ideas, and discussing financial concepts, I began engaging with subjects that went far beyond the classroom curriculum. The magazine exposed me to perspectives, debates, and questions that made economics feel relevant to the real world. For a long time, I experienced that process as a contributor. My responsibility was simple: produce good work. If I wrote a strong article, I had succeeded. If I missed something, the consequences were relatively limited. Becoming Editor-in-Chief changed that completely. Suddenly, my responsibility was no longer just my own work. It was the quality of the entire publication.

What fascinated me most was how different ownership feels from participation. As a contributor, it is easy to focus on individual performance. As a leader, your attention shifts toward systems. Instead of asking whether one article is strong, you start asking whether the publication consistently produces strong articles. Instead of focusing on your own writing, you focus on helping other writers improve theirs. The challenge becomes less about personal output and more about creating an environment where others can succeed. That transition was far more difficult than I expected because it required a completely different mindset.

This revealed a contradiction that exists in many organizations. People often assume that the most talented individual naturally becomes the best leader. Sometimes that happens, but the skills are not identical. Being a strong writer does not automatically make someone a strong editor. Being a strong contributor does not automatically make someone a strong leader. Leadership requires a shift in perspective. Success is no longer measured by your own achievements. It is measured by the achievements of the people around you. The role becomes less visible, but often more important.

Another lesson came from realizing that every organization develops a culture, whether intentionally or unintentionally. As Editor-in-Chief, I was not just responsible for content. I was helping shape expectations. What standards would be accepted? How seriously would research be taken? Would the magazine prioritize quality over quantity? These decisions seem small in isolation, but over time they influence how people approach their work. Culture is often invisible when it is functioning well. You only notice it when it begins to weaken. That made me appreciate how much leadership is about setting standards that others gradually adopt.

Of course, there was also pressure. Every edition would inevitably be compared to previous ones. Expectations were higher because I was no longer contributing from the sidelines. I was responsible for the final product. Initially, that pressure felt intimidating. Over time, however, I began to see it differently. Expectations are often a sign that people trust you with responsibility. They believe you are capable of maintaining and improving something that matters. Once I viewed pressure through that lens, it became less of a burden and more of a privilege.

Looking back, becoming Editor-in-Chief taught me something that extends far beyond publishing. Leadership is not a destination you reach after proving yourself. It is a transition into a different kind of responsibility. The title may look impressive from the outside, but the real challenge begins after you receive it. You stop focusing on your own success and start focusing on the success of the system you are responsible for. That shift changed how I think about leadership entirely. Becoming Editor-in-Chief was not important because of the position itself. It was important because it taught me that true ownership begins where recognition ends.
